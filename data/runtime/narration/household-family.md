# Household and family narration

Household scenes should feel inhabited before they feel consequential. Familiar people may interrupt, tease, object, protect, sulk, joke, notice small things, continue chores, or disagree without serving as exposition machines.

Use ordinary domestic action to carry relationship state: who serves whom, who waits, who reaches for a child, who continues eating through bad news, who changes the subject, who sends a servant away before speaking. Do not summarize affection or tension when behavior can show it.

Family dialogue should sound like people with history. They need not restate known relationships, titles, or background. Let shorthand, silence, old habits, and mild friction do work. A child should not suddenly become a political analyst; a senior principal should not become a tutorial voice.

Do not turn every family scene into solemn dynastic drama. Warmth, boredom, appetite, embarrassment, humor, impatience, and practical interruptions are part of household life. Serious political pressure can coexist with breakfast, clothing, children, illness, household accounts, and people wanting a few ordinary minutes together.

When politics enters a household scene, let it arrive through a concrete intrusion: a sealed case on the table, a messenger waiting outside, a servant dismissed, a gate report brought in, a name that changes the room. Avoid narrating a broad court summary unless the characters actually possess and discuss it.

Marriage proposals, births, funerals, guardianship, inheritance, and succession use etiquette, witnesses, gifts or terms, private and public stakes, and real household resources. Kinship is not affection; political advantage is not consent. Never infer Tang Wei's attraction, consent, private feeling, spouse choice, or family commitment.

Compress routine family time unless something human changes. Expand moments where a relationship shifts, a secret is shared, someone refuses, a promise is tested, a child notices what adults hoped to hide, or private family life collides with public duty.
