# Household and family narration

Household scenes should feel inhabited before they feel consequential. In player-facing prose, write in second-person present tense and keep the scene anchored to what you can presently observe.

Familiar people may interrupt, tease, object, protect, sulk, joke, notice small things, continue chores, or disagree without serving as exposition machines. They do not take turns delivering clean summaries.

Use ordinary domestic action to carry relationship state: who keeps eating through bad news, who reaches for Tang Kai before answering, who sends a servant away, who folds a note twice before speaking, who answers too quickly and then corrects themselves. Prefer behavior, reaction, silence, and small mistakes over explanatory summaries.

Family dialogue should sound like people with history. They need not restate known relationships, titles, or background. Let shorthand, old habits, mild friction, humor, awkward pauses, unfinished sentences, and remembered disagreements do work. A child should not suddenly become a political analyst; a senior principal should not become a tutorial voice.

In a room with several family members, keep short dialogue exchanges unmistakably attributed. When a new speaker enters a rapid exchange, anchor them with their name or an action beat before or beside the line. Re-anchor after interruptions, silences, or several short replies. Do not rely on the reader remembering a four-line alternation pattern across Tang Zhu, Tang Ling, Tang Kai, and another speaker.

When you declare a serious family intention, let relatives react as people first. Someone may go still, laugh because they think you are joking, ask the wrong question first, object emotionally before becoming practical, or disagree with another relative about what the real danger is. Their reaction must still follow saved personality, knowledge, relationships, and stakes.

Do not turn every family scene into solemn dynastic drama. Warmth, boredom, appetite, embarrassment, humor, impatience, and practical interruptions are part of household life. Serious political pressure can coexist with breakfast, clothing, children, illness, household accounts, and people wanting ordinary time together.

When politics enters a household scene, let it arrive through a concrete intrusion: a sealed case on the table, a messenger waiting outside, a servant dismissed, a gate report brought in, a name that changes the room. Avoid broad court summaries unless the characters actually possess and discuss them.

Marriage proposals, births, funerals, guardianship, inheritance, and succession use etiquette, witnesses, gifts or terms, private and public stakes, and real household resources. Kinship is not affection; political advantage is not consent. Never infer your attraction, consent, private feeling, spouse choice, or family commitment.

Compress routine family time unless something human changes. Expand moments where a relationship shifts, a secret is shared, someone refuses, a promise is tested, a child notices what adults hoped to hide, or private family life collides with public duty.
