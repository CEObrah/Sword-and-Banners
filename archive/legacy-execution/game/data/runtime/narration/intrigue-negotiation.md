# Intrigue, contracts, and negotiation narration

Intrigue should be legible through **evidence, access, timing, and behavior**, not through omniscient declarations that someone is plotting.

Tension comes from information asymmetry, leverage, deadlines, witnesses, credibility, payment, law, security, and the possibility that someone simply refuses. Keep rumor, inference, suspicious pattern, corroborated report, and proof distinct in both dialogue and narration.

Reveal political or intelligence information through concrete channels: a courier who arrived late, two sources whose accounts disagree, a seal used outside normal procedure, a road patrol doubled without public notice, a merchant quietly rerouting carts, an official who refuses to put an instruction in writing. Do not turn an intelligence scene into a bullet-point risk register.

Briefings should have source texture. A good intelligence officer distinguishes what was seen, who reported it, how old it is, what is missing, and what conclusion remains only an inference. Let uncertainty create choices instead of filling gaps with narrator certainty.

Negotiations should be conversational contests over real terms. People may evade, anchor high, demand witnesses, split issues, threaten to leave, ask for time, test credibility, offer partial concessions, or refuse. Merchants and mercenaries care about terms, risk, reputation, opportunity cost, and enforcement. Political or commercial success should emerge from valid leverage rather than charisma as magic.

Do not repeatedly explain every party's incentives after each line of dialogue. Show the consequence of wording through pauses, counteroffers, changed demands, who is willing to sign, and what becomes possible or impossible.

When hidden world state is not yet lawfully known to Tang Wei, narrate only the traces that reached him. Never use dramatic irony to leak the true plot merely because the repository knows it.
